\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Shared passed sets in explicit-state model checking.}
Stern and Dill~\cite{Stern01} partitioned the state space across processors and
routed successors to their owner; multi-core \textsc{Spin}~\cite{Holzmann07} and
\textsc{DiVinE}~\cite{Barnat12} refined that shape for shared memory, and
\ltsmin{}~\cite{Laarman10,Kant15} replaced it with a single lockless hash table
that every worker inserts into, removing the routing phase.  Our striped passed
set follows that choice of a shared structure over a static partition.  What does
not carry over is the operation: a lockless hash table implements
insert-if-absent, which is idempotent and pure in its effect, whereas
\cref{alg:update} retires and replaces stored zones.  This is why the engine uses
a lock per stripe and a shared/exclusive split rather than a compare-and-swap on
a slot, and why \cref{thm:probe} has to be proved.

\paragraph{Parallel and distributed reachability for timed automata.}
Behrmann et al.~\cite{Behrmann00,Behrmann05} distributed \uppaal{}'s search and
observed that the number of explored states \emph{grows} when the search is
parallelised, because a state covered in one order is expanded in another: on
their \textsc{Dacapo} model the count rose from $45\,001$ on one node to
$110\,634$ on seventeen, and was recovered to $52$k only by ordering the waiting
list by depth.  That effect is a property of subsumption rather than of their
distribution, so it reappears here, and it is the reason the engine keeps strict
level synchronisation rather than a free-running worklist.
Dalsgaard et al.~\cite{Dalsgaard12} combine the \opaal{} front end with
\ltsmin{}'s shared table to obtain a multi-core timed-automaton reachability
tool, and identify the interaction between subsumption and a shared table as the
central difficulty.  Our contribution to that point is \cref{lem:monotone} and
its consequences: what makes a shared subsumption test usable without validation
is that coverage is monotone, and once that is available the read-mostly nature
of the operation can be exploited directly.

\paragraph{When parallelism costs states.}
The effect above is worth isolating, because it bounds what parallelism can be used for here.
Behrmann et al.~\cite{Behrmann00} report \textsc{Dacapo} rising from $45\,001$ stored states on one
node to $110\,634$ on seventeen, purely from distributing a search that uses subsumption: a state
that one order finds covered, another order expands.  The same wall is visible from the other side
in our own measurements --- \uppaal{}'s three search orders give identical stored counts on
\code{fischer}, \code{viking} and \code{csma}, depth-first is $31.6\%$ worse on \code{phil}, and
\code{fischer8} is bit-identical from $1$ to $12$ threads \emph{because} strict level
synchronisation is enforced rather than in spite of it.  The rule we draw from this, and follow
throughout, is that additional workers may be spent on redundant but independently sound work, and
may not be spent on relaxing the order in which states are inserted: the first is free, the second
is paid for in states.

\paragraph{Distributed timed model checking.}
The \textsc{Zeus} line~\cite{Braberman05,Braberman06} is the sustained attempt at
distributing timed verification, built over \textsc{Kronos} and computing
predecessors backwards from the target rather than successors forwards.  Its
engineering effort went almost entirely into problems created by distributed
memory: partitioning the state space across nodes, detecting termination across
them, and predicting and redistributing workload on the fly.  Shared memory
dissolves most of that.  There is one heap, so ownership and message passing are
not needed to make a state visible to another worker; termination is the emptying
of one frontier rather than a distributed snapshot; and load balancing is a
work-stealing schedule over a shared queue.  What survives the change of setting
is not their architecture but their measurements --- the search-order effect
above --- and the observation that a backward search terminates without
abstraction, which is orthogonal to the direction taken here.

\paragraph{Subsumption for liveness.}
Subsumption is a safety-only licence.  A subsumption step replaces a zone by a
larger stored one, so the resulting graph over-approximates the runs of the
automaton, and an accepting cycle in it need not be realised by any
run~\cite{Tripakis09}.  Emptiness for timed B\"uchi automata therefore requires
either a lifting argument~\cite{Tripakis09,Li09} or a \emph{restricted}
subsumption relation whose graph contains no cycle carrying both an accepting
state and a subsumption edge~\cite{Herbreteau12b}, and the problem is strictly
harder than reachability unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$~\cite{Herbreteau16}.
Laarman et al.~\cite{Laarman13} give the multi-core version.  The liveness
queries this engine answers, $\code{E[]}\,\phi$ and $p \leadsto q$, are weaker
than full B\"uchi acceptance --- their propositions are functions of the
discrete state alone --- which is what makes subsumption usable here at all.

\paragraph{Subsumption and antichains.}
Storing sets of incomparable elements under a subsumption preorder rather than
individual elements is the antichain technique of De Wulf et al.~\cite{Wulf06}.
For timed automata the relevant preorders are the $\preceq_{LU}$-based
abstractions of Behrmann et al.~\cite{Behrmann06} and Herbreteau et
al.~\cite{Herbreteau12}, implemented by \uppaal{} and
\textsc{TChecker}~\cite{Herbreteau13}; extrapolation~\cite{Daws98,Behrmann06} is
the older route to finiteness, and the engine chooses between the two per run
rather than per tool.  Termination arguments for antichain-based searches
normally rest on the stored set being an antichain, which convex merging breaks;
\cref{thm:term} replaces that argument with one that bounds insertions by
abstraction classes using only monotonicity of coverage.

\paragraph{Compact zone representations.}
Storing zones as minimal constraint systems is due to Larsen et
al.~\cite{LarsenLPY97}.  The representation is chosen for bytes resident, and
the cost it moves is the cost of a query, which must rebuild a dense matrix.
\Cref{lem:minclosure} avoids the rebuild for the query that dominates by deciding
inclusion against the generators, and the dense mirror buys the dense form back
only for the stored sets whose measured query traffic pays for it.

\paragraph{Determinism.}
Blelloch et al.~\cite{Blelloch12} argue for internally deterministic parallel
algorithms, whose intermediate state does not depend on the schedule.  This
engine is not one, and cannot cheaply be made one, because convex merging is not
confluent; \cref{thm:verdict} isolates what is deterministic --- the verdict and
the set of covered symbolic states --- and every mechanism is checked against
that property rather than against reproducibility of the passed set.
